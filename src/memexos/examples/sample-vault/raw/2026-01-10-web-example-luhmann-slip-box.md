# Luhmann's slip-box, explained

Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998) was a German sociologist, known for a theory of society as a system of communications.
He was also unusually productive, publishing dozens of books and hundreds of articles, and he credited much of that
to a single tool: his *Zettelkasten*, or slip-box.

## What it was

Over more than four decades Luhmann filled wooden drawers with about 90,000 index cards. Each card held one idea,
written in his own words, never a copied quotation. Cards were not sorted by topic. A new card was filed next to the
card it continued, and its number recorded that position: after card 21/3 came 21/4, and a side thought on 21/3 became
21/3a. Because numbers never changed, a card could be referenced from anywhere in the box.

Those references are what made the box work. Luhmann wrote on a card the numbers of other cards it related to, and
kept a short keyword index that pointed to a few entry cards per subject. Following references from an entry card led
through chains of related ideas, often to connections he had not planned.

## "Communication with slip-boxes"

In a 1981 essay, *Kommunikation mit Zettelkästen*, Luhmann described the box as a partner in conversation: because it
was organised by links rather than by a fixed outline, consulting it would return combinations of ideas that surprised
its owner.

After his death the box went to Bielefeld University, where the Niklas Luhmann archive has been digitising the cards
and publishing them online.

## The method today

Sönke Ahrens' book *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017) made the method popular among students and writers. Its core
advice: write short notes in your own words while reading, then turn the ones worth keeping into permanent notes that
stand on their own and link them to existing notes. Writing is then a matter of following and arranging what is
already in the box.
